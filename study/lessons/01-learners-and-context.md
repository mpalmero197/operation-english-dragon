## Learning aims

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. Describe how adult learners’ cultural, linguistic, and educational backgrounds shape classroom behaviour and needs  
2. Distinguish types of motivation and explain practical ways to sustain engagement  
3. Match teacher roles to lesson stages and contexts  
4. Make informed, sensitive choices about varieties of English and the role of L1  
5. Draft notes you could later expand into a *Focus on the learner* assignment

## Core concepts

### 1. Adult learners bring a “backpack”

Every adult walks in with:

- **Cultural norms** (e.g. how to address a teacher; comfort with pair work; views on error correction)  
- **Linguistic background** (monolingual or multilingual; standard/non-standard varieties; script differences)  
- **Educational history** (grammar-translation school; communicative courses; interrupted schooling; literacy level)  
- **Life roles** (parent, shift worker, professional) that affect energy, homework time, and goals  

**Teaching implication:** Needs analysis is not a luxury. Even a short “about you” questionnaire or first-lesson mingle gives you data for planning.

### 2. Motivation (keep it practical)

| Type (useful classroom labels) | Example | What you can do |
|--------------------------------|---------|-----------------|
| **Instrumental** | Needs English for a visa, job, or exam | Link tasks to real outcomes; clear success criteria |
| **Integrative** | Wants to join English-speaking communities | Social tasks, cultural content chosen *with* learners |
| **Intrinsic** | Enjoys the challenge / language play | Curiosity hooks, choice of topics, games with purpose |
| **Extrinsic** | Family pressure, course requirement | Short wins, visible progress charts, respectful rapport |

Motivation fluctuates. Sensitivity + variety + relevance beat “motivational speeches.”

### 3. Learning preferences ≠ fixed “styles” labels

Adults often prefer some activity types (discussion, quiet reading, drills, discovery). Prefer:

- **Variety** across a lesson and week  
- **Choice** when possible (“Task A or B”)  
- Awareness that preferences are influenced by past schooling — not destiny  

Avoid rigid “visual/auditory/kinaesthetic” stereotypes as the main planning tool.

### 4. Teacher roles (shift during a lesson)

| Role | Typical moment |
|------|----------------|
| **Planner / organiser** | Before class; setting up tasks |
| **Informer / knower** | Clarifying meaning, form, phonology |
| **Facilitator** | Pair/group work; circulating |
| **Monitor** | Listening for language and task success |
| **Diagnostician** | Noticing patterns of need |
| **Feedback provider** | After tasks; delayed correction |
| **Rapport builder** | Always — tone, names, fairness |

CELTA tutors often comment when trainees stay stuck as “informer” for the whole lesson.

### 5. Context: physical, online, and resources

Ask: Who are the learners? How many? Level mix? Room layout or platform? Materials available? Time of day? Institutional constraints?

Adapt: online may need clearer ICQs, breakout management, and screen-share discipline; low-resource contexts need boardwork, paper, and learner-generated content.

### 6. Varieties of English

English is plural (e.g. British, American, Indian, Nigerian, Singapore varieties; English as a lingua franca). For CELTA:

- Be **accurate** about the model *you* use in class  
- Be **respectful** of learners’ goals and local norms  
- Teach **intelligibility** and awareness, not “one accent only” snobbery  

### 7. Multilingualism and L1

L1 is a resource, not the enemy. It can:

- Support meaning (quick translation of a hard word)  
- Cause transfer errors (false friends, word order, phonology)  

Use L1 judiciously according to centre policy and learner preference; never shame it.

## Key terminology glossary

| Term | Working definition (CELTA-level) |
|------|----------------------------------|
| **Needs analysis** | Gathering information about learners’ goals, levels, preferences, and constraints to inform planning |
| **Learner profile / class profile** | Summary of who is in the room (levels, L1s, motivations, issues) |
| **Rapport** | Constructive, respectful relationship that supports learning |
| **Instrumental / integrative motivation** | Learning for practical goals vs. desire to join a community/culture |
| **Teacher role** | Function the teacher takes at a stage (e.g. facilitator, monitor) |
| **Variety (of English)** | A regional or social form of English with its own norms |
| **L1 / L2** | First language / additional language being learned |
| **Transfer** | Influence of L1 (or other languages) on L2 production/reception |
| **Inclusivity** | Planning so learners with diverse needs can participate |
| **Context** | The setting (learners, place/mode, resources, institution) |

## Worked examples (original)

### Example A — Mini class profile (B1 evening class, mixed L1)

- 12 adults: 5 Spanish, 3 Arabic, 2 Mandarin, 2 Polish  
- Goals: 6 work email/meetings; 4 travel/social; 2 university prep  
- Preferences stated: like speaking; dislike long grammar lectures  
- Challenges: late arrivals after work; Arabic speakers request more spelling support; one learner mentions mild hearing difficulty — prefer facing the speaker, written key words on board  

**Planning moves:** Front-load speaking with clear models; board target language; pair strong/weaker writers carefully; keep instructions visual + oral; finish with a “takeaway phrase” for work emails.

### Example B — Motivation dip

Learner completes homework sporadically. Chat reveals night shifts.  
**Response:** Negotiate shorter, high-value homework; offer in-class consolidation; celebrate small wins; avoid public shaming.

### Example C — Variety choice

Coursebook uses British spelling (*organise*, *colour*). Learners mostly consume US media.  
**Response:** Stick to one model for *productive* spelling in this course for consistency; raise awareness of US variants receptively; be transparent (“In this class we write *organise*; you’ll also see *organize*.”).

## Practice tasks

**Task 1.** Label each motivation as mainly instrumental, integrative, intrinsic, or mixed — and suggest one classroom response.  
1. “I need IELTS 6.5 for nursing registration.”  
2. “I love English songs and want to understand the lyrics.”  
3. “My partner’s family speaks English; I want to feel part of dinners.”  

**Task 2.** For each stage, name a suitable **teacher role**:  
(a) Learners discuss weekend plans in pairs.  
(b) You clarify the difference between *I’ve been to* and *I went to*.  
(c) You notice half the class misunderstood a listening gist task.  

**Task 3.** Short case: A multilingual A2 group includes one learner whose L1 uses a non-Roman script. List **three** inclusive moves for the first two weeks.  

**Task 4.** Draft a 120–150 word **Focus on the learner** style paragraph about an imagined learner “Samira” (L1 Arabic, B1, needs English for hotel reception work, strong speaking confidence, spelling anxiety). Include: background, needs, one suggested activity + rationale.

## Answer key

**Task 1**  
1. Instrumental → exam-focused practice, timed tasks, clear band descriptors in plain language.  
2. Intrinsic (with integrative flavour) → lyric gap-fills, pronunciation of song lines, learner-chosen tracks.  
3. Integrative → role-plays of family chat; cultural pragmatics (polite disagreement, compliments).  

**Task 2**  
(a) Facilitator / monitor. (b) Informer / knower (clarifying language). (c) Diagnostician → then organiser (re-teach or adjust task) / feedback provider.  

**Task 3** (any three sensible):  
Board key vocabulary with clear handwriting; allow phone dictionary photo of board; pair with supportive peer; teach Roman-script letter formation briefly if needed; give written homework models; avoid only oral instructions.  

**Task 4** *(sample — yours may differ)*:  
Samira is a B1 adult learner whose L1 is Arabic. She works toward front-desk hotel English: greetings, checking guests in, handling simple complaints. She is outgoing in speaking tasks and builds rapport quickly, which supports fluency practice. However, spelling and sound–letter relationships cause anxiety, especially with vowel letters that do not map neatly from Arabic. A useful activity is a short reception role-play paired with a “key phrases” spelling checklist (e.g. *reservation*, *available*, *passport*), followed by peer dictation of those items. This targets her occupational need while giving safe, focused spelling practice without reducing speaking confidence.
